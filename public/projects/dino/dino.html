Self-supervised SE(3)-invariant image embeddings

Introduction

Data labeling is a notoriously expensive bottleneck in computer vision tasks. This has driven significant interest toward Self-Supervised Learning (SSL). SSL is a paradigm where models learn useful visual representations without manual annotations Among recent SSL methods, DINO [1] has gained popularity due to its ability to produce high-quality, semantically meaningful embeddings by contrasting multiple augmented views of an image.

Typically, DINO achieves this by generating multiple random crops of a single image, encouraging the model to learn invariances to such cropping transformations. However, real-world visual changes often arise from three-dimensional viewpoint variations rather than mere 2D cropping. Inspired by this insight, our project explores whether SSL can leverage true 3D viewpoint changes to learn robust and invariant image embeddings.

To this end, we replace the traditional random-crop augmentations used by DINO with viewpoint variations provided by the CO3D dataset. By training the DINO framework with these realistic 3D viewpoint shifts, we aim to learn embeddings that are explicitly invariant to transformations in the special Euclidean group, SE(3).

Prior Work

Representation Learning

Representation learning involves discovering meaningful and generalizable features from raw data. Historically, supervised learning has been a primary method for representation learning, using labeled datasets to train models that map inputs directly to annotated outputs. Models trained with supervision, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), learn representations implicitly while optimizing for classification accuracy. However, supervised methods rely heavily on large, meticulously annotated datasets, which can be expensive and impractical for many real-world applications.

In contrast, Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) addresses this issue by removing the dependency on labeled data. SSL methods generate their own supervisory signals from the data itself, typically through cleverly designed pretext tasks or contrastive objectives. This approach allows models to learn robust and generalizable embeddings without manual annotations. Remarkably, SSL methods have achieved and surpassed the performance of supervised learning approaches on several standard benchmarks, such as image retrieval or copy detection [1].

Self-Supervised Learning

Recent SSL methods broadly fall into two categories: contrastive and non-contrastive.

Contrastive SSL methods, like SimCLR [2] and MoCo [3], train models by pulling representations of augmented views of the same image closer together in the embedding space while pushing representations of different images apart. SimCLR generates positive pairs through random autmentations and uses a large batch size for negative sampling, which is computationally expensive. MoCo addresses the computational bottleneck of SimCLR with a momentum encoder and a queue-based dictionary of embeddings.

Non-contrastive methods eliminate the need for negative pairs, allowing training to work with fewer resources. Notable non-contrastive SSL approaches include BYOL [4] and DINO [1]. BYOL relies on two networks, a target and an online network, where the online network is trained to predict the target network's representation of an image under a different augmentation. DINO uses a similar architecture to BYOL, but uses a different loss, which avoids collapse through centering and sharpening strategies applied to the teacher's outputs. We use DINO for our analysis because of its high quality embeddings and its robustness to collapse.

Multi-View Augmentation in SSL

Leveraging multi-view information for SSL is a promising yet relatively underexplored domain. For instance, VideoMoCo [13] leverages temporal coherence in video frames to construct positive pairs, training models to remain consistent across time. Similarly, Time-Contrastive Networks [14] use temporally adjacent frames from videos to learn embeddings that are invariant to time-based transformations. However, these methods often focus on temporal consistency as opposed to explicit 3D viewpoint variation.

Background on DINO

DINO is a simple self-supervised method for learning image embeddings. The method pairs especially well with Vision Transformers (ViT), and results in features with more explicit knowledge of semantic segmentation than similar models trained with supervision. The resultant embeddings are also immediately useful as k-NN classifiers.

Like in knowledge distillation, two networks, a student and a teacher, are fed two different random views x 1 and x 2 of the same underlying image x . They each output a K -dimensional feature vector, and a softmax function is applied to both outputs. The student is trained to match a centered and sharpened version of the teacher's distribution via backpropagation against a cross-entropy loss. A stop-gradient prevents training of the teacher. This can be viewed as learning to optimize an K -way classification task that is defined implicitly through the training procedure, even though we cannot directly interpret the K classes and no labels are used for supervision.

Unfortunately, we do not already have a strong teacher model that we can train our student's embeddings off of. Instead, the teacher model is constructed from the student model via a momentum encoder. The student and teacher are constrained to have the same architecture, and the teacher parameters are updated with an exponential moving average (ema) of the student parameters. Essentially, the loss function teaches the student to match outputs with an averaged version of its older selves on two different views of the same image.

A diagram of the DINO framework. Image taken from the DINO paper.
The views for the two models are generated using a multicrop strategy. The set contains two global views (larger crops of the image) and several local views (smaller crops of the image). All crops are passed to the student, while only global crops are passed to the teacher, encouraging learning of "local-to-global" correspondences. Intuitively, this should teach the student model that local patches of an image (e.g. part of a chair) should have a similar embedding to global patches of an image (e.g. the entire chair).

We used the so-called "basic parameterization" of DINO, where global crops cover more than 50 % of the area of the image and are rescaled to a resolution of 224 2 , while local views cover less than 50 % of the area of the image and are rescaled to a resolution of 96 2 . Note that a few additional augmentations are applied to the views, such as flipping, color jitter, Gaussian blurring, and solarization, encouraging invariance with respect to these image transformations.

A common problem in self-supervised learning techniques is preventing collapse. For example, one way for the DINO model to minimize the cross-entropy loss above is to just always output the same distribution no matter the input, but this wouldn't result in a useful image embedding. One common way of avoiding collapse is via a contrastive loss, i.e. pushing negative pairs apart in addition to pulling positive pairs together. In DINO, however, it can be prevented simply by centering and sharpening the momentum teacher output. The center is computed as an exponential moving average of the means of the teacher output across recent batches. This is subtracted off the output as a bias term. Sharpening is done by exponentiating the values of the distribution and renormalizing, using a small temperature under 0.1 .

Datasets

Common Objects in 3D (CO3D)

The Common Objects in 3D (CO3D) [5] dataset was originally designed for category-specific 3D reconstruction and novel-view synthesis. It consists of approximately 1.5 million frames from 19,000 videos, categorized into 50 object classes.

Example videos from the CO3D dataset. Taken from the CO3D repo.


We chose the CO3D dataset for training our modified DINO model because it provides diverse views of numerous objects. CO3D also includes labeled categories, which allows us to use it for evaluation as well.

For training the DINO model, we used a subset of the CO3D dataset containing 7177 video sequences. These sequences were divided into 5998 sequences for training and 1179 sequences for testing. Since the original dataset's frames come from continuous videos, viewpoint changes between adjacent frames are minimal. Thus, we downsampled each sequence to 10 evenly spaced frames. Note that when training, we ignore the object labels of the sequences.

CIFAR-100

While testing on a held-out portion of the CO3D dataset is useful for assessing in-distribution performance, it has limitations. The CO3D test set shares the same 51 object categories present in the training set and generally consists of images with a centered object in the foreground. As a result, this setup may not fully reflect how well the learned representations generalize to broader, more diverse data distributions.

To address this, we also evaluated our models on the CIFAR-100 dataset [6], a standard benchmark in image classification. CIFAR-100 contains 60,000 32x32 color images across 100 classes, with 600 images per class. The dataset is split into 50,000 training and 10,000 test images. Unlike CO3D, CIFAR-100 includes varied and less structured compositions, making it a useful benchmark for evaluating how well learned representations transfer to out-of-distribution visual inputs.

Methods

We trained DINO models using three different types of data augmentations: view, crop, and both. These augmentation settings control how input samples are prepared for the teacher and student networks during self-supervised training:
  • view: In this setting, augmentations are based purely on viewpoint changes. For each sequence in our dataset, we use all 10 available views. Two views are randomly assigned to the teacher network, and the remaining eight are assigned to the student network. Teacher images are resized to 256 pixels and center-cropped to 224x224. Student images are resized to 128 pixels and center-cropped to 96x96. This setting leverages real-world 3D transformations to drive invariance.
  • crop: This follows the original DINO augmentation setup. For each sequence in the training data, a single viewpoint is randomly sampled from the 10 viewpoints in the sequence. Then, we apply the standard DINO augmentations to the selected viewpoint, including the global and local crops. Specifically, global crops are sampled with a scale range of 0.4 to 1.0, and local crops with a scale range of 0.05 to 0.4. The augmentations are then resized and fed into the teacher or student networks accordingly. This setup serves as a baseline, so that we can compare the traditional DINO augmentation with the viewpoint augmentation when trained on CO3D images.
  • both: This is a hybrid approach that combines viewpoint-based augmentation with cropping. First, two of the 10 available views are randomly assigned to the teacher network, and the remaining eight to the student network—just as in the view setting. Then, additional random cropping is applied: the teacher views receive global crops, and the student views receive local crops.
To assess the effect of model capacity on performance, we trained each of the three augmentation settings using two different model architectures:
  • vit_tiny: A small Vision Transformer with 3 attention heads and a 192-dimensional output embedding.
  • vit_small: A larger variant with 6 attention heads and a 384-dimensional output embedding.

Evaluation

To assess the quality of the learned image representations, we conducted two main types of evaluation: k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) classification and linear probing. Both evaluations were done on both the CO3D and CIFAR-100 datasets. For CO3D, we randomly sample 1 of the 10 images in each sequence to use in the evaluation. The CO3D dataset allows us to evaluate the performance of the embeddings on a test set drawn from the same distribution as the training data. CIFAR-100 provides a challenging domain shift evaluation, as its small, low-resolution images and broad semantic categories differ significantly from CO3D.

These methods help us understand both the immediate utility of the learned embeddings (without any fine-tuning) and their effectiveness when adapted to downstream classification tasks with minimal supervision.

k-Nearest Neighbors

The k-NN classifier provides a non-parametric way to evaluate how well the embeddings represent the semantic meaning of an image. It requires no additional training, and thus, directly measures how well the learned features cluster semantically similar examples.

To perform k-NN, we first split the evaluation dataset into training and test sets. We extracted embeddings for all images in both sets using the trained DINO model. For each test embedding, we computed its cosine similarity (via dot product) with all training embeddings. The top-k most similar training embeddings were then used to perform a weighted vote based on similarity scores. The predicted class for each test sample was determined from this vote. This is the method used in [7].

We report Top-1 accuracy, which reflects the proportion of test samples where the top-voted class is correct, and Top-5 accuracy, which measures whether the correct label is within the five most confidently predicted classes.

Linear probe

Linear probing is another common benchmark used to evaluate learned representations. The core idea is that a strong embedding space should support simple, downstream classifiers. In this setup, we freeze the backbone encoder (the DINO-trained ViT model) and train a linear classifier on top of the extracted embeddings. This linear layer is trained using the training split of the evaluation dataset, and performance is measured on the corresponding test set. The goal is to measure how linearly separable the learned features are. A strong representation should allow a simple linear model to achieve high accuracy on downstream classification tasks.

The view-based models perform best, especially for the vit_small architecture, suggesting that the learned embeddings are not overly tailored to the CO3D distribution. Instead, they retain generality and remain the most effective when applied to visually and structurally different datasets, indicating robustness across domains.

Together with the k-NN results, this provides further support that viewpoint-aware SSL not only benefits in-distribution performance but also offers a degree of robustness in domain-shift scenarios like CIFAR-100.

These findings support the hypothesis that viewpoint-aware SSL leads to improved image embeddings, and these improvements remain effective across datasets with different characteristics, indicating that the model captures transferable, domain-agnostic features.

We believe that viewpoint transformations may result in models that are more likely to only attend to the object of interest, since the object is what always remains in frame across the video. Conclusion

Our evaluations indicate that explicitly leveraging 3D viewpoint variations in SSL improves model performance on downstream evaluation tasks, such as k-NN and linear probe classification. Across all evaluations, the view-based models achieved the highest performance. Generally, the crop-based models performed the worst.

The both configuration, despite using a combination of the augmentations, shows inconsistent benefits—possibly because combining viewpoint changes with random cropping introduces too much variability or misalignment, which can dilute the useful learning signal.

Limitations and future work

Despite promising results, this work comes with several limitations. First, our models were trained on a dataset with only 51 object categories, limiting the diversity of learned features. Second, due to limited compute, we only experimented with two model scales and did not tune hyperparameters extensively.

Another challenge is scalability: while our method of viewpoint augmentation produces better results on certain tasks, the method relies on having access to a high-quality dataset of many different views of many objects. In practice, this may be difficult to collect for large, diverse, and high-quality datasets.

Additionally, there's some ambiguity around what qualifies as "multi-view" data and what makes it useful. On one hand, structured datasets explicitly capturing multiple viewpoints around an object—such as CO3D—offer a clear view of each viewpoint. On the other hand, general video frames may include arbitrary camera motion or scene dynamics, making it unclear whether they truly provide useful viewpoint variation, though they may better reflect real-world conditions.

One potential direction to address challenges related to data availability is leveraging synthetic data. Recent advances in differentiable rendering (e.g. [8], [9], [10]) and generative modeling (e.g. [11], [12]) could allow for the creation of diverse, high-quality synthetic multi-view datasets. Exploring synthetic data could enable training SSL models like DINO with extensive, carefully controlled viewpoint variations, as well as more diverse objects and locations. This approach could significantly mitigate data bottlenecks and scale viewpoint-based SSL effectively to broader, more diverse visual domains.
